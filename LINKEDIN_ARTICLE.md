# Whisper at 1.58 bits: it got more accurate — and here is everything that broke on the way

Ternarisation is usually discussed in the context of large language models:
BitNet, b1.58, billions of parameters. The logic is clear — that is where
compression pays the most.

I took something smaller. **whisper-small, 242 million parameters** — speech
recognition. Next to a seven-billion-parameter LLM this looks like an evening's
work: thirty times fewer parameters, a well-understood architecture, nothing
exotic.

It took considerably more than an evening. And that, I think, is the actual
result — **not the numbers, but how many places this can break.** So I am going
to walk the whole path: where I stumbled, which diagnoses I got wrong, where I
got in my own way, and how I caught it.

Code and weights are open, links at the end. If you spot a mistake, tell me —
this is my first time doing this.

**On "1.58 bits":** that is log₂(3), the information content of a ternary
alphabet, and it is the name BitNet gave the idea. My actual storage is
**2.125 bits per weight** — two bits per code plus one fp16 scale per 128
weights. BitNet's real files are the same way. I use the recognisable term and
give you the honest number in the same breath.

---

## What came out

**192 weight matrices of whisper-small contain only −1, 0 and +1. The model is
more accurate than the fp16 version it was distilled from.**

| LibriSpeech, greedy decoding | ternary | stock whisper-small |
|---|---|---|
| test-clean | **2.7145 %** WER | 3.3391 % |
| test-other | **7.3817 %** WER | 7.5002 % |
| long-form, 9 recordings | **3.632 %** | 3.891 % |

Both test sets are genuinely held out — neither was used in training.

198,180,864 weights, 82 % of the model, **carry no magnitude at all**: each is
one of three values, plus one fp16 scale per 128 inputs. The runnable file is
140 MiB instead of 465, and it works on a CPU.

## The path

### Step 1. You cannot just round

The obvious move is to take the weights and round each to the nearest of three
values. This does not work, and the reason is simple: **weights are not equally
important.** A weight multiplied by a large activation affects the output more
than one sitting on an activation near zero. Rounding knows nothing about that.

So the codes are initialised by a projection that minimises the
**activation-weighted** error: for each group of 128 inputs, we solve for the
combination of codes and scale giving the smallest error *given what those
weights are actually multiplied by*. The activation statistics are collected in
advance with a calibration pass.

### Step 2. Quantisation-aware training

Then QAT with a straight-through estimator. The forward pass uses hard ternary
codes; the gradient flows backward into a latent fp32 weight. Plus distillation:
KL against a bf16 teacher, cross-entropy on labels, and feature matching.

All standard. The trouble did not start here.

### Step 3. The model learned to loop

A few hours into training, the model started repeating phrases in circles.

The cause was in the data. Labels were generated by the teacher, and **some of
the teacher's own labels contained runaway repetitions — up to 108 repeated
5-grams in a single label.** In total, repetitions in the labels were 14× more
frequent than in the ground-truth transcripts. The model dutifully learned them.

The share of bad labels was small. But a bad signal can be **small in proportion
and enormous in concentration** — one label with a hundred repetitions outweighs
a hundred clean ones.

Five hours of training wasted. The rule now runs before every training job:

```
repetitions  share of labels with repeated 5-grams, and their total count
length       median / p99 / max in tokens — outliers are runaway labels
punctuation  share of labels with marks and with capitals
prefix       first 5 tokens match the expected format
duration     median / p99 / max
by eye       print 5 labels in full and read them
```

One minute of checking against hours of waste. **The dataset gets checked before
training, every time.**

### Step 4. The wall at thirty seconds

The model worked well on short clips and fell apart on long audio.

The diagnosis turned out to be almost absurdly narrow. Whisper always receives a
30-second window; if the audio is shorter, the remainder is zero-padded. When
the audio is **exactly 30.000 seconds**, there is no zero tail at all — and the
fine-tuned model had never seen that input.

The check that proved it: feed 29.99 seconds — that is, leave **one frame of
silence** — and everything works perfectly. One frame.

The fix is data: build a corpus where a third of the windows are exactly 30.000
seconds. Plus a hard rule — **never cut audio during assembly.** A window ends at
an utterance boundary; if the next utterance does not fit, the window closes.

### Step 5. One thousand percent WER

Here I made the most instructive mistake of the project.

QAT keeps a latent fp32 weight that the gradient flows into. The reasoning
seemed sound: the latent weight is the "real" weight, the codes are its
projection, so for export you re-project from the latent weights.

**Result: 1000.56 % WER.**

Diagnostics showed the latent weights had drifted to 3.8× the median, and 60 %
were saturated. They had long since stopped being weights and become a gradient
accumulator. **The latent weight is a scratchpad, not a master copy. The master
is the trained (codes, scales) pair** — the thing that was actually optimised.

I suspect most people who write their own QAT and export walk into this one.

## Where I got in my own way

This is probably the most useful section: diagnoses I stated confidently and got
wrong.

**"It's the prefix."** I explained a discrepancy between two measurements by
claiming one of them wasn't passing the language tokens. It sounded convincing.
I **had not opened the file**, where those tokens were already being passed two
lines above. The real cause was duller and more important: nondeterminism in
batched matrix multiplication. Batch 16 gives 3.4298 %, batch 8 gives 3.4224 %,
batch 1 gives 3.4168 % — on identical code.

That mistake produced something useful: a **harness noise floor of ±0.013 pp.**
Anything under 0.02 pp is not signal. Before that I was seriously discussing
differences in the third decimal.

**"The artefact is symmetric."** I measured one side of an effect, got 1.36 pp,
and declared the other side the same. The other side was 4.02 pp. A headline
quality figure had to be honestly restated from 96 % down to 70 % and 87 % on
two different cuts.

**"That run never reached 36k steps."** I wrote it into a results file. Then I
opened the training log: 36000.

An uncomfortable pattern connects all three: **every error made the picture
tidier.** That is not haste — haste would err in both directions. It is the pull
toward seeing what fits the story. The only remedy that worked: **if a check
would complicate the narrative, run it first, not last.**

More from the same drawer:

- **"1.48× faster"** — I compared a CUDA-graphs variant against a non-graphs
  variant. Different question; the measurement was void.
- **A memory benchmark read 991 MiB** — a leftover dictionary was holding
  756 MiB of dead latent weights. The real figure was 228 MiB.
- **A units bug in my own theory column** (an extra divide by 1024) hid the main
  performance finding for hours.

Since then I write **predictions before the measurement**: a file stating what
each outcome would mean — success, partial, failure, alarm threshold. It has
twice stopped me from fitting the explanation to the result after the fact.

## Step 6. Getting onto a CPU

Next: run this without a GPU. This got interesting.

ggml **already had** a `Q2_0` type: 2.25 bits, group of 64 — a perfect container
for ternary codes. Lucky, it seemed. Except:

- the quantisation tool listed it as **unsupported**, so the path was
  unreachable;
- **there was no SIMD kernel on x86** — the scalar fallback made the encoder 11×
  slower than fp16;
- quantising the original model into that type produced pure garbage.

The type had been declared and **never once run end to end.** I wrote the missing
AVX2 kernel (encoder 12.5 s → 2.4 s per window) and removed the two gates.

**Another of my own mistakes, and a galling one.** The first build produced
"almost right but disfigured" text: the opening words correct, then decay. The
cause was my exporter pulling 287 non-quantised tensors from the **stock** model
— tensors that had been trained for 80,000 steps in mine. A chimera: our ternary
matrices bolted to someone else's scaffolding. Worst of all, **I had already made
this exact mistake in another script and failed to carry the lesson over.**

**And separately, the head.** Whisper ties the decoder's token embedding to the
output projection. That is 18 % of the parameters and 86 of the 140 MiB — by far
the largest thing still in fp16. So I quantised it too.

**WER: 2.71 % → 1152 %.** Not degradation, total collapse: 563,054 spurious
insertions. That tensor was never trained under quantisation and does not
survive two bits. Now I have a number instead of a hunch.

## Speed, and why it is not obvious

Same weights, same audio, batch 1:

| | H200, PyTorch fp16 | CPU, ternary |
|---|---|---|
| decode / token | 8.3 ms | **6.0 ms** |
| encoder / 30 s window | 7.1 ms | 550 ms |

The decoder is faster on the CPU than on an H200. This is not a story about CPUs
being secretly great — it is about what autoregressive decoding at batch 1
actually is: **439 kernel launches per decoder pass, roughly 15 µs each,
regardless of matrix size.** The card spends its time launching, not computing.

The encoder tells the opposite story: a dense matmul over 1500 frames is exactly
what GPUs exist for, and there the H200 wins by 77×.

The lesson generalises past this model: **before optimising a kernel, find out
what you are bound by** — compute, memory, or launches. I spent time optimising
something that was not the bottleneck.

## What does not work

A compression result with no failure list means nobody looked hard enough. Mine:

- **No punctuation.** Commas appear in 0.0–0.7 % of utterances depending on
  decoding mode; stock whisper-small manages 63.3 %. Capitals: 2 % against 98 %.
  The cause is my labels, lowercased and stripped of punctuation. I have already
  built the punctuated data and have not yet run the training.
- **English only.** Russian degrades 12.6×. But an fp16 control trained the same
  way degrades identically, so the cause is monolingual fine-tuning, **not**
  ternarisation. Those are different accusations and I am careful not to swap
  them.
- **The encoder on CPU is still 2× slower than fp16** in wall-clock terms.
- **One mystery is unsolved.** Model loading takes 950 ms instead of 150. I
  formed three hypotheses — lookup table, redundant buffer, pointer aliasing —
  and **all three were wrong**: a control `memset` of the same size into the same
  memory takes 4 ms, while my loop takes 826 ms. I still do not know why. I am
  saying so plainly, because an open problem is also a result.

And the honest version of the headline. "Better than stock whisper-small"
answers "is this model good", not "what did quantisation cost" — my model was
also fine-tuned. So I ran an **fp16 control**: same code path, same data, same
schedule, quantisation switched off.

**The gap holds at about 0.37 pp and does not narrow. Neither branch reached a
floor** — both were still falling almost linearly when the runs ended. Whether
the ternary branch eventually reaches the same floor is **unproven**, and I say
that straight rather than implying otherwise.

## Try it yourself

```bash
hf download armanibadboy/whisper-small-ternary ggml-small-wal-ternary-q2_0.bin
./whisper-cli -m ggml-small-wal-ternary-q2_0.bin -f audio.wav -ng
```

The repo shows how to **verify for yourself**, in four lines of PyTorch, that
every matrix really does hold at most three distinct values per group — rather
than taking my word for it.

- **Model:** huggingface.co/armanibadboy/whisper-small-ternary
- **Code, recipe and all measurement files:** github.com/AubakirovArman/whisper-ternary

Everything is published: the library, the data pipeline, training, the exporter,
the whisper.cpp patch, and **the logs of every measurement** each number above is
read from. Including the negative results and the fp16 control.

## Why I am writing this

There is almost no public information on taking a speech recognition model to
ternary weights and keeping the quality. That was the main reason to publish the
whole thing rather than just the flattering number.

And second: the task *looked* easy. Small model, familiar architecture, nothing
unexplored. **In practice it tripped at nearly every step** — in the data, in the
model's decoding modes, in the export, in other people's libraries, and most
often in my own hasty explanations. If you are about to attempt something
similar, I hope this list saves you a few days.

I am doing this for the first time. If you see something done wrong, or a
simpler way, please tell me. I would especially like to compare notes with
anyone working on low-bit quantisation, ggml kernels, or on-device ASR.
